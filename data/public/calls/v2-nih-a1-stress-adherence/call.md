## Purpose and award conditions

The R21 supports exploratory or developmental research at an early conceptual stage. It can
support a risky idea, an unfamiliar model or population, or a new use of an existing method
when the potential scientific gain justifies the uncertainty. A small version of an
established, incremental research program is not the intended use of this mechanism.
Preliminary data are **not required**. A persuasive rationale may rest on published research;
any applicant-generated results included should be described with their limitations.

## Scientific emphasis and expected outcomes

Most research on missed and mistimed doses treats medication-taking as a fairly stable patient characteristic explained by knowledge, beliefs, cost or regimen complexity. Less often examined, and seldom at the time scale on which doses are taken or missed, is whether day-to-day stress and lapses of attention disrupt an established routine. A related question is whether these processes matter more after a hospital stay has changed the regimen and interrupted home habits. This emphasis supports exploratory observational and measurement work. The work should establish whether these processes can be measured often enough and with acceptable burden to be related to medication-taking, and whether the associations found justify larger studies.

Address all of the following outcomes:

- Nursing and behavioral researchers know whether within-person changes in stress, attention or both precede omitted, delayed or duplicated doses among adults managing ongoing regimens, and how consistent those associations are across people.
- Investigators know whether stress, attention or both, according to the proposed pathway, can be assessed repeatedly with acceptable burden and tolerable missing data in the population and setting studied; for post-discharge studies, this includes the weeks when regimens change and home routines are re-established.
- Teams planning larger studies know how closely the available objective and self-reported sources of dose-level medication-taking data agree in the population studied.
- Teams planning larger studies know whether assessment notifications, monitoring devices, safety communications and other study contact change the medication-taking they are intended to observe.
- Teams planning larger studies know how the study's mechanism and medication-taking measures perform among older adults and among people with cognitive or sensory limitations.
- Adults whose routines are most easily disrupted, such as people taking many medications, people with mild cognitive limitations and people who live alone or, in post-discharge studies, are discharged home alone, are represented in mechanistic evidence and are not excluded because of assessment burden.
- Investigators and funders can decide, from feasibility results and effect estimates with their uncertainty judged against the study's progression criteria, whether a stress or attention pathway warrants a larger observational study or later intervention development, or should be set aside.

A medication routine is the repeated, often cue-dependent sequence by which a person takes scheduled doses. Stress may use up the resources needed to carry out the routine or displace the cues that trigger it. Attention and prospective memory determine whether an intended dose is remembered at the right moment. After hospital discharge, many patients go home with new, stopped or changed medications, reduced function and altered daily schedules, so the routine has to be rebuilt at a time when stress may be high. Studies may start from nursing science, for example transitional care, symptom management or self-management. They may also start from health, cognitive or clinical psychology, for example habit formation, prospective memory or stress appraisal.

Proposals should use observational designs, secondary analysis of existing data, or measurement-development and feasibility work, and should not assign participants to interventions. A proposal may focus on stress, on attention, or on their interplay. The weeks after hospital discharge are of particular interest; another setting can be proposed where it better tests the mechanism. Each proposal should establish whether the proposed mechanism can be measured at the required time scale and whether it is related to medication-taking within persons over time, and should end with evidence that supports a decision about further study.

- Specify a theoretical model, from nursing or psychological science, linking stress, attention or both to a defined feature of the medication routine, such as dose timing, consistency or omission.
- Measure medication-taking at the level of individual scheduled doses, so that each dose can be classified as taken on time, delayed, omitted or duplicated.
- Study the weeks after hospital discharge, recording regimen changes, discrepancies and who manages medications at home, or justify another setting where regimen or routine changes test the mechanism, and record those changes.
- Analyze within-person associations separately from between-person differences, with lag structures fixed in advance and adjustment for time-varying confounders such as pain, sleep, symptom burden and regimen changes.
- Assess recruitment, retention and the completeness of medication-taking data, reporting reasons for refusal, withdrawal and device non-use where data are newly collected.
- Examine whether associations differ by age, sex, cognitive status, number of medications and living arrangement, presenting these analyses as exploratory where the sample does not support formal tests.
- State progression criteria for proceeding to a larger observational or mechanistic study, fixed before new data collection or, for secondary-data analyses, before those analyses, disclosing any prior access to the existing data.

Not supported under this notice:

- Clinical trials as defined by NIH are not allowed.
- Pilot or feasibility testing of adherence supports, such as reminder apps, alerting pill bottles, pharmacist follow-up calls, stress-management or attention training, in which participants are assigned to them to see whether medication-taking changes.
- Monitoring devices or smartphone applications that give participants medication-taking reminders, missed-dose or adherence alerts, or adherence feedback during the observation period. Prompts to complete stress or attention assessments and narrowly defined safety communications are not covered by this exclusion.
- Cross-sectional adherence surveys that assess stress or attention once and cannot place them in time relative to medication-taking.
- Comparisons of drug efficacy, dosing or formulation, and pharmacokinetic or drug-interaction studies.
- Evaluation of discharge processes, medication reconciliation programs or hospital quality-improvement initiatives as the primary aim.
- Engineering of new ingestible sensors, smart packaging or drug-level assays as the main objective, rather than evaluating existing measures for the proposed question.
- Studies whose primary aim is the clinical diagnosis of dementia, delirium or attention-deficit disorders rather than the examination of attention as a process in medication-taking.

Topic-specific requirements:

- Critically appraise the rigor of the published or unpublished evidence supporting the proposed research.
- Justify the sample size for the proposed unit of analysis.
- Describe the measures used to control bias in the proposed study.
- Specify how the collected data will be analysed to answer the research questions.
- Describe contingency approaches for the main anticipated threats to feasibility.
- Where applicable, explain how relevant biological variables such as sex and age enter the design and analysis, with scientific reasons for restrictions.
- Where applicable to human-participant research, justify the study population’s inclusion across sex/gender, race, ethnicity and age.
- In the Introduction to Resubmission, explain how the revised application responds to the previous review through substantive changes or reasoned rebuttals.
- This notice adds a restriction that NIH resubmission policy does not impose: it accepts only A1 applications whose unfunded A0 asked whether stress, attention or both act as mechanisms of medication-taking. State the A0 central question. Aims, population, setting, measures and design may be substantially revised in response to the review, provided the revised application still addresses that question; an unrelated project is not eligible under this notice.
- Define the medication-taking outcome operationally: the primary data source, the observation window, the treatment of scheduled versus as-needed medications, and the handling of prescriber-directed starts, stops and dose changes, including any made at hospital discharge.
- Justify the timing and frequency of assessments of stress, attention or both, according to the proposed pathway, relative to scheduled doses, so that the proposed mechanism is measured before the doses it is expected to affect.
- Explain why each study procedure, including assessment notifications, monitoring devices, safety communications and any return of study information to participants or clinicians, does not prospectively assign participants to an intervention, and how the content and delivery times of assessment notifications are kept from functioning as medication reminders.
- Within the Approach, describe risks to participants and the protections for them: assessment burden and distress during illness or recovery, capacity to consent, privacy of device and smartphone data, and a written procedure for safety communications to participants or clinicians that states the triggering criteria, who is contacted and how quickly, and that excludes routine adherence feedback.
- For each measure of the selected mechanism and of medication-taking, state whether it is established, adapted or new, and what existing evidence supports its reliability and validity in the intended population and setting.

## Required research narrative

Submit one Markdown document with these three main sections, in order:

### Introduction to Resubmission

In at most 600 words (one page), summarise substantial additions, deletions and changes.
Answer the major criticisms in the supplied summary statement, pointing to the actual
revisions in the new aims, design or plan. Where disagreeing, give a reasoned, evidence-based
rebuttal. Explain the changes relative to the earlier application and review report.
Do not mark changes by highlighting, colour, bold or italics in the revised Research Strategy
or other attachments; describe them here. Normal structural headings are permitted.

### Specific Aims

State the research problem, the central question or hypothesis, the objectives, expected
findings, and their potential scientific contribution. Keep the aims appropriate to an
exploratory award and explain what can be learned even if the main hypothesis is unsupported.

### Research Strategy

Use the following child headings in this order:

- **Significance:** Explain the unresolved problem and why the proposed advance matters.
  Evaluate strengths and weaknesses of the prior evidence, including applicant-generated
  results where used. Distinguish an established observation from an inference that this project will test.
- **Innovation:** Explain the conceptual or methodological advance relative to current
  research. Identify the advantage of the proposed application of the methods; using a recent
  technique is not by itself a novelty argument.
- **Approach:** Describe the design, methods, collection and analysis of data, interpretation,
  comparisons or controls, and rationale for the sample size and unit of analysis. Address
  weaknesses in the supporting evidence, measures against bias, relevant biological variables,
  anticipated difficulties, alternatives, and criteria for success. For an immature method,
  explain how feasibility will be established and risk managed within the award period.
  Explain the personnel roles and resource access needed to execute the design. Identify risks to participants or
  hazards and the protections appropriate to them.

Address sex and other relevant biological variables, including age where pertinent, in design
and analysis. Justify restrictions scientifically. If human subjects are proposed, the
scientific design should be consistent with appropriate inclusion of women, racial and ethnic
minorities, and people across the lifespan. Detailed enrollment and protection forms belong
elsewhere in an actual NIH application.

Only the Introduction, Specific Aims and Research Strategy with its three children are required.
A reference list may follow. Budgets, biosketches, facilities attachments, human-subjects forms,
vertebrate-animal attachments, authentication plans, and data-sharing plans are outside this
narrative deliverable.

## Review criteria

This notice uses the NIH Simplified Review Framework, applicable to R21 due dates from
25 January 2025 onward. Lower numeric scores indicate stronger assessments.

| Review element | Assessment | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Factor 1: Importance of the Research (Significance, Innovation) | 1–9 | Value of the question and potential advance; soundness of the supporting evidence and rationale |
| Factor 2: Rigor and Feasibility (Approach) | 1–9 | Reliable and unbiased inference; suitable comparisons, sample sizes and analysis; for human-participant research, inclusion plans appropriate to the scientific goals; achievable work and credible management of uncertainty |
| Factor 3: Expertise and Resources | Sufficient / gaps identified; no numeric score | Whether the proposed team and environment can do this work, judged in relation to career stage and the actual tasks; explain any gaps |
| Overall Impact | Individual reviewer score 1–9 | Integrated judgment of the project's potential lasting influence, considering all three factors and applicable additional review criteria |

Overall Impact is not a prescribed arithmetic average. The exploratory purpose matters: lack of
preliminary data alone is not a defect, and ambitious uncertainty should be assessed alongside
the possible scientific gain. Inclusion plans are assessed within Factor 2. Protections for
human subjects, vertebrate-animal welfare and biohazards are Additional Review Criteria when
applicable. As a
resubmission, the application is also evaluated as now presented, taking into consideration
the responses to the previous review's comments and the changes made to the project; this
narrative must describe a scientifically and ethically coherent design, not reproduce those
forms.

## Length contract

NIH permits **1 page for Introduction to Resubmission**, **1 page for Specific Aims** and **6 pages for Research Strategy**, with the latter
limit shared by Significance, Innovation, and Approach. The page allowances are not transferable.
Over-limit applications are not accepted for review; excess narrative is not simply ignored.

**Benchmark conversion:** use **600 words per page**, giving **600 words for Introduction**, **600 words for Specific Aims**
and **3,600 words for Research Strategy**, with a **4,800-word combined ceiling**.
Exceeding any section ceiling or the combined ceiling makes the submission
**inadmissible**, with zero tolerance. Tables, captions, and in-text citations count; the proposal title and
reference list do not.

This is a benchmark word contract, not an NIH word-count rule. Its format basis is US Letter,
Arial 11 pt, 0.5-inch margins, at most 15 characters per inch and six lines per vertical inch;
600 words is a practical narrative estimate per page.
