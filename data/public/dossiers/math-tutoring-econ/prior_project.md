# Final Report: High-Dosage Math Tutoring in Urban Middle Schools

**Spencer Foundation research grant (fictional award PB-SP-MTE-2020-01)**
Principal Investigator: Rachel M. Ostrander, Velmarden University
Co-Principal Investigator: Marcus J. Treviño, Velmarden University
Project period: 1 August 2020 – 31 July 2024
Total award: $748,000

Report submitted: October 2024

---

## 1. Overview

This project evaluated the impact of a high-dosage mathematics tutoring programme on the achievement, attitudes and course-taking trajectories of students in grades 6–8 in the Cedarquill Metropolitan School District (CQMSD), a large urban district serving a predominantly low-income, racially diverse student population. The study combined a cluster-randomised controlled trial (RCT) with a qualitative investigation of tutor practices and experiences.

The project was funded entirely by the Spencer Foundation. The $748,000 grant supported the research and evaluation activities: research staff salaries, data collection, instrument development, qualitative fieldwork, and dissemination. The actual RatioWeave-Tutor tutoring programme—tutor salaries and benefits, training, materials, and supervision—was funded by CQMSD using a combination of district operating funds and federal Elementary and Secondary School Emergency Relief (ESSER) allocations. The project was not funded by the National Science Foundation and does not constitute NSF-supported research.

## 2. Programme Description

The tutoring model, called RatioWeave-Tutor, provided three 45-minute sessions per week of small-group tutoring (3 students per tutor) during the regular school day, replacing a non-academic elective period. Tutors were full-time, salaried paraprofessionals with at least a bachelor's degree, who received 80 hours of initial training and weekly coaching from a district mathematics coach. The curriculum was aligned to the district's adopted middle-grades mathematics sequence and emphasised conceptual understanding, procedural fluency and problem-solving.

## 3. Research Design

### 3.1 Randomised Controlled Trial

- **Unit of randomisation:** School (to avoid within-school contamination).
- **Sample:** 42 schools were eligible; 38 agreed to participate. Schools were stratified by baseline proficiency rate (above/below district median) and percentage of students receiving free or reduced-price lunch (above/below 80%), then randomly assigned within strata: 19 treatment, 19 control.
- **Student sample:** 4,217 students in the analytic sample (2,118 treatment; 2,099 control) after attrition. The original enrolled sample was 4,530 students; attrition was 6.9%.
- **Demographics of analytic sample:** 47% Black, 31% Hispanic, 12% White, 10% other; 83% eligible for free or reduced-price lunch; 14% receiving special education services; 19% classified as English learners.
- **Outcome measures:** (a) State mathematics assessment scaled scores (primary outcome, administered spring of each year); (b) Researcher-developed assessment of algebraic reasoning (secondary outcome, pre/post each year); (c) Mathematics self-efficacy scale (Likert, 12 items, α = 0.88).
- **Analytic approach:** Intent-to-treat (ITT) estimates from two-level hierarchical linear models (students nested in schools), with randomisation-stratum fixed effects and baseline covariates (prior-year math score, gender, race/ethnicity, FRL status, EL status, special education status). Effect sizes reported as Hedges' g.

### 3.2 Qualitative Component

Semi-structured interviews with 48 tutors (25 in Year 2, 23 in Year 3), purposively sampled across schools and experience levels. Interviews lasted 45–75 minutes and were audio-recorded and transcribed. Analysis used a priori and emergent codes in NVivo, with inter-rater reliability (Cohen's κ) of 0.81 across two coders (Treviño and a trained doctoral student).

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Impact on Mathematics Achievement

After two years of treatment (the primary pre-registered endpoint):

- **State math assessment:** Treatment students scored higher by **0.21 SD** (Hedges' g = 0.21, 95% CI [0.09, 0.33], p = 0.001). The effect was statistically significant at the 0.05 level.
- **Algebraic reasoning assessment:** Treatment effect of **0.28 SD** (95% CI [0.14, 0.42], p < 0.001).
- **Mathematics self-efficacy:** Treatment effect of **0.13 SD** (95% CI [0.01, 0.25], p = 0.04).

Subgroup analyses (pre-registered):

- Students in the bottom quartile of baseline math scores: effect on state assessment of **0.34 SD** (95% CI [0.16, 0.52]).
- Hispanic students: **0.26 SD** (95% CI [0.10, 0.42]).
- Black students: **0.19 SD** (95% CI [0.05, 0.33]).
- English learners: **0.24 SD** (95% CI [0.06, 0.42]).

### 4.2 Attendance and Dosage

Among treatment students, the median tutoring attendance rate was **87%** of scheduled sessions over two years. In an exploratory comparison of the 1,614 treatment students attending at least 80% of sessions with the control sample, the adjusted difference was **0.27 SD** (95% CI [0.13, 0.41]). Attendance was not randomised; this selected-sample association does not identify a causal effect of higher attendance or establish an effect larger than the ITT estimate.

### 4.3 Cost

The per-student cost of the RatioWeave-Tutor programme was **$3,400 per year**, including tutor salaries and benefits, training, materials and supervision. This cost was borne by CQMSD; the Spencer Foundation grant covered only the research and evaluation. The per-student cost figure does not include the opportunity cost of the replaced elective period.

### 4.4 Qualitative Findings

Four themes emerged from tutor interviews:

1. **Responsive pacing.** Tutors described adjusting lesson pace based on real-time observation of student work, but reported difficulty diagnosing specific misconceptions quickly, especially in rational number operations and proportional reasoning.
2. **Relationship building.** Tutors emphasised that consistent, small-group interaction built trust, which they saw as essential for students to reveal confusion.
3. **Curriculum rigidity.** 31 of 48 tutors (65%) felt the scripted curriculum limited their ability to pursue productive student thinking.
4. **Isolation.** Tutors had limited interaction with classroom teachers; 38 of 48 (79%) reported meeting with the student's regular math teacher fewer than twice per semester.

## 5. Limitations

1. The study was conducted in a single urban district; generalisability to suburban or rural settings is unknown.
2. The control condition was business-as-usual instruction; we cannot isolate which components of the tutoring model (small group size, dosage, curriculum alignment, tutor training) drove the effects.
3. We measured outcomes only through the end of the two-year treatment period; longer-term persistence of effects is unknown.
4. The qualitative sample of 48 tutors, while purposive, may not capture the full range of tutor experiences.
5. The study did not incorporate any technology-based or AI-assisted tutoring tools; all instruction was delivered face-to-face by human tutors without adaptive software.

## 6. Data and Materials
