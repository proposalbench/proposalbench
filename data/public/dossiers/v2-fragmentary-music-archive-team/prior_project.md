# Final report: Fragmentary Music Evidence Pilot

Award RU-CE-2024-07, Rannakaar University Research Development Fund

Project period: 1 January 2024–31 December 2025. Final report accepted 20 February 2026. This copy incorporates the permissions and deposit status recorded on 18 September 2026; the analytical results are unchanged.

Principal investigator: Dr Maarja Sirelkoit. Team: Dr Tõnis Lehevald, historical linguistics; Kadri Vahenurm, acoustic signal processing.

## Purpose and material

The pilot examined whether incomplete musical sources could be described without concealing disagreements between readers. Its practical outputs were a linked catalogue, independent classifications, provenance records and a small set of documented performance examples.

The corpus comprised 180 score fragments from six manuscript folders, with 30 fragments selected from each folder. Collection descriptions assign the source material to approximately 1870–1935. Those dates are catalogue ranges, not independently verified dates for every leaf. Selection required permission to reproduce the fragment and enough surviving notation for an initial reading. Completely illegible leaves were therefore absent from the sample.

Each fragment received a stable identifier linked to its physical folder and leaf reference. Separate records described visible notation, surviving words, physical loss and editorial inference. Fragment identifiers refer to physical source units. Several fragments can preserve related material, and similarity between them does not establish a shared author or date.

The audio component comprised 24 consented examples performed by eight adult participants, with three examples per participant. All 24 examples are unaccompanied solo singing with underlaid text: one singer performs a single melodic line in each recording. Each deposited example lasts 45 seconds, giving 18 minutes of deposited audio. Participants performed short readings prepared from the source material. These are contemporary demonstration recordings of the team's interpretations. The deposited reading sheets distinguish surviving words and syllables from supplied text and record uncertain placement of syllables beneath notes.

## Annotation and provenance methods

Sirelkoit and Lehevald independently assigned one of three labels to each fragment: predominantly vocal, predominantly instrumental, or insufficient evidence. The guide required readers to cite visible features supporting their choice. Surviving verbal text could support a vocal classification, but its absence was not sufficient evidence of instrumental use. The insufficient-evidence label was available whenever the surviving material did not support either positive classification.

The readers entered their labels before seeing each other's decisions. Subsequent discussion was recorded separately and did not overwrite the independent records. The project did not appoint an adjudicator, assemble a gold-standard set or ask a third reader to settle disagreement. Interpretive notes distinguish a surviving inscription from an inferred language identification and distinguish both from a modern transcription.

The team completed source links and separate observation/inference fields for all 180 catalogue entries. This is a documentation-completeness result. Verification of the underlying provenance statements and classifications remained outside that check.

## Classification results

The two annotators agreed on 146 of 180 classifications, or 81.1%. A descriptive Wilson 95% confidence interval for this proportion is 74.8%–86.2%, treating fragments as independent observations. Because fragments were selected within folders and can be related, that interval does not capture all dependence or selection uncertainty and is not a population-level estimate for the whole collection.

The agreed classifications comprised 96 predominantly vocal fragments and 50 predominantly instrumental fragments. There were no cases in which both readers selected insufficient evidence. The remaining 34 fragments were disputed: 18 had vocal/instrumental disagreement, 10 had vocal/insufficient-evidence disagreement and six had instrumental/insufficient-evidence disagreement. These categories account for the entire corpus.

The 34 disputed cases remain disputed in the deposited catalogue. They are not 34 demonstrated errors, and the 146 agreements are not 146 verified correct answers. Agreement was an observed property of these two readers using this guide.

Reading meetings identified recurring reasons for disagreement, including detached verbal text, notation resembling both a sung line and an instrumental part, and uncertainty about whether marks belonged to the original writing or a later addition. These observations were qualitative explanations of particular cases. The project did not estimate their prevalence across the wider archive.

## Audio processing results

Vahenurm implemented a reproducible desktop workflow for importing the licensed recordings, retaining untouched source files, generating denoised derivatives and logging processing settings. All 24 examples passed through the workflow. The deposited audio format is mono PCM WAV at 48 kHz and 24-bit resolution. File checksums distinguish each original from its derivatives.

Noise reduction was assessed in manually marked non-performance intervals within the same recordings. The median reduction in measured noise power was 3.2 dB across 24 examples. A performer-cluster bootstrap gave a 95% interval of 2.4–4.0 dB for the median; the resampling unit was the performer, retaining the three associated examples together. With only eight performer clusters, this interval provides limited information about variation beyond these recordings.

No reference annotation of note onsets exists for any of the 24 recordings as of 18 September 2026. The interval markings identify non-performance regions only; the reading sheets have no alignment to recorded event times. Untouched recordings preserve the performed interpretations, rather than establishing the historical correctness of the readings.

This measure concerns intervals without intended performance. Note recognition, preservation of musical transients and recovery of masked material were not evaluated. Informal listening informed settings but was not a blinded perceptual evaluation. There was no held-out test set or predetermined recovery pass/fail rule. Recovery of missing material was outside the pilot's aims.

## Delivery, expenditure and limitations

The final accepted expenditure was EUR 46,800: EUR 31,200 in staff costs, EUR 6,000 in performer fees and recording-session support, EUR 4,800 in collection handling and digitisation services, and EUR 4,800 in institutional indirect costs. These amounts exhaust the award. No equipment purchase was charged to it. The internal award specified a fixed indirect-cost contribution of EUR 4,800 against EUR 42,000 in direct costs; it did not use the university's external-grant percentage rate.

The project delivered the catalogue, annotation guide, processing scripts, consent register and restricted audio deposit. The public metadata deposit contains the independent labels and provenance fields. Audio access follows the separate performer agreements.

The pilot established that the team could process and document the selected material with existing facilities. It left the central interpretive problem unresolved: plausible readings can differ even when their supporting evidence is recorded. The sample excluded the most illegible material, the performers were recruited locally, and the recordings were made for demonstration.
