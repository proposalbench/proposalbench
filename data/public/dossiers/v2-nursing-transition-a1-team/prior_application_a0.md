## Specific Aims (as submitted)

The first weeks at home after a hospital stay change how adults take medications: regimens are altered at discharge, routines are interrupted, and follow-up visits, symptoms and household demands compete for attention. Nurses see patients who know their regimen yet miss doses during this period. We propose that stress and lapses of attention during the transition disrupt medication routines, and that these processes can be observed in daily life.

We will follow 90 adults discharged home from medical units, recruited at two outpatient clinics, for six weeks, using smartphone ecological momentary assessment (EMA) of stress, a brief mobile attention task and medication diaries. The study is observational; no intervention is delivered.

**Aim 1.** Describe the course of perceived and momentary stress, attention lapses and self-reported medication taking over the first six weeks after discharge.

**Aim 2.** Estimate the association between momentary stress in the first week after discharge and self-reported medication adherence at week 6. *Hypothesis:* higher person-mean stress on days 1–7 is associated with lower week-6 adherence (r of -0.30 or stronger).

**Aim 3 (exploratory).** Examine whether attention lapses statistically account for part of the stress–adherence association.

The results will indicate whether stress and attention should be measured, and later addressed, in nursing care after discharge.

## Preliminary data (as submitted)

Phase 1 diary pilot at Ridgeline Transitional Care Clinic (CRU College of Nursing Pilot Research Award, 2024–2025): 31 adults enrolled and 26 (83.9%) completed six weeks. EMA prompt completion was 76.0% and mobile PVT completion 78.8% on days 1–7. Person-mean momentary stress correlated with week-6 self-reported adherence at r = -0.31 (95% CI -0.62 to 0.09; n = 26). The intraclass correlation of momentary stress was 0.48, indicating substantial within-person variation in the first week.

## Approach (shortened)

**C.1 Design.** Prospective observational cohort; two sites; six-week follow-up from discharge.

**C.2 Participants.** Adults aged 18 years or older discharged home from a medical unit within the previous 7 days, with at least 3 scheduled daily oral medications, English-speaking and able to use a smartphone (loaners provided). Exclusions: discharge to a skilled nursing facility, diagnosis of dementia, hospice enrollment.

**C.3 Recruitment.** 90 participants over 18 months: 45 at Ridgeline Transitional Care Clinic (CRH) and 45 at Lakevale Community Health Center, which 'has agreed in principle; a letter will follow'. Ridgeline enrolled 31 participants in 7 months during Phase 1.

**C.4 Measures.**
- Baseline: demographics, number of scheduled medications, PSS-10.
- Days 1–7 (EMA burst): four prompts per day rating momentary stress (0–10); one 3-minute mobile PVT per day (lapses = responses slower than 500 ms).
- Medication taking: evening diary on days 1–7 (doses taken, missed, late); one structured recall interview at week 6 covering missed doses in the previous 7 days.
- Week 6: PSS-10.

**C.5 Analysis.** Aim 1: descriptive statistics and linear mixed models for EMA data. Aim 2: linear regression of week-6 adherence on person-mean stress (days 1–7), adjusted for age, number of medications and baseline PSS-10. Missing data handled by multiple imputation under a missing-at-random assumption; 20% attrition expected. Aim 3: product-of-coefficients estimate with bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals, reported as exploratory.

**C.6 Sample size.** With 90 enrolled and 20% attrition (72 analyzable), the study has 80% power at two-sided alpha 0.05 to detect r = 0.33.

**C.7 Potential problems and alternatives.** Self-report may overstate adherence; we chose diaries and recall because electronic monitoring adds cost and participant burden during the transition. If EMA completion falls below 70%, prompts will be reduced to three per day.

**C.8 Timeline.** Months 1–3 start-up; months 4–21 enrollment and follow-up; months 22–24 analysis and dissemination.
