# CALDERVANE Ireland: final scientific report

**Indoor environment and respiratory symptoms in social housing**  
University of Carraventh; principal investigator Dr Néavessa Kelvarneth  
Grant DHHRF-2020-061; reporting period 1 January 2021–31 December 2025  
Final report accepted by the funder on 27 March 2026  
Record and output status checked: 30 September 2026

## Purpose and study design

CALDERVANE Ireland examined indoor temperature, ventilation-related indicators, particulate matter and visible mould alongside respiratory symptoms in occupied social housing. It also evaluated changes associated with a landlord-led deep-renovation programme. Renovation allocation and construction schedules were controlled by housing providers, not investigators. The study was observational and did not randomise households or prescribe clinical treatment.

Recruitment and baseline measurements took place in 2022. Renovations were completed during 2023, and follow-up measurements were undertaken in 2024. Data reconciliation and analysis continued through 2025. The programme covered Dublin, Cork and Limerick, with recruitment through participating providers and direct consent from occupants. The sample was geographically distributed but was not nationally representative.

## Participants and renovation exposure

At baseline, 300 dwellings contributed 672 participants: 432 adults and 240 children. The adults included 156 people aged at least 65 years. Among the children, 96 had parent-reported physician-diagnosed asthma. These vulnerability groups were defined before analysis; their inclusion did not imply adequate power for every subgroup comparison.

At follow-up, 276 dwellings remained, representing 92% dwelling retention. They contained 612 participating occupants: 396 adults and 216 children, including 144 older adults and 88 children with asthma. The remaining 24 dwellings were lost through moves, withdrawal or inability to arrange follow-up. Participants who left were not replaced.

Of the original dwellings, 104 underwent deep renovation and 196 did not undergo deep renovation during the observation period. Follow-up included 96 renovated dwellings and 180 comparison dwellings. The corresponding losses were eight and sixteen dwellings. Renovation packages combined fabric insulation, window replacement, airtightness work and mechanical extract ventilation, with package details recorded from completion certificates. Some comparison dwellings received minor maintenance. Heating remained dwelling-level; no participating site had a district heating connection.

## Environmental and health measurements

Each dwelling received three temperature and relative-humidity loggers, located in the main living room, principal bedroom and a second occupied room where available. This required 900 loggers. A separate carbon-dioxide monitor was installed in each dwelling, requiring 300 monitors. Logging intervals were five minutes. Sixty optical particulate-matter monitors rotated through a nested sample of 120 dwellings, with fourteen-day deployments at baseline and follow-up; 108 dwellings supplied valid paired particulate records.

Temperature analyses used calibrated readings and prespecified completeness thresholds. Summer bedroom overheating was defined as hours above 26 °C between 22:00 and 07:00 during June–August. This provided 828 possible monitored night-hours per summer. The threshold was an exposure definition, not a clinical diagnosis or a claim of universal regulatory compliance. Valid baseline summer records were available for 264 dwellings; 246 dwellings had valid summer records at both measurement waves.

Inspectors recorded visible mould using a standard room checklist and photographs reviewed without access to symptom responses. Carbon dioxide was treated as an indicator affected by occupancy and ventilation, not as a direct measure of ventilation rate. Optical particulate readings were corrected against collocated reference measurements; their precision did not support chemical source attribution.

Health outcomes included wheeze during the previous four weeks, nights disturbed by respiratory symptoms and asthma-control questionnaires appropriate to participant age. A nurse recorded medication use and recent respiratory infection. Spirometry was offered as a secondary assessment, but incomplete attendance and variable acceptability prevented its use as the principal longitudinal endpoint. Referral decisions remained separate from research outcome coding.

## Main findings

At baseline, the 264 dwellings with valid summer data had a mean of 118 bedroom overheating hours, with a household-bootstrap 95% confidence interval of 104–132 hours. The distribution was right-skewed. The mean should not be interpreted as the experience of a typical individual or extrapolated to an entire calendar year.

Visible mould was recorded in 84 of 300 baseline dwellings, or 28%. In the baseline analysis of 672 occupants, visible mould was associated with four-week wheeze: adjusted odds ratio 1.62, 95% CI 1.13–2.32. The model accounted for household clustering and adjusted for age, smoking exposure, tenure duration, season of assessment and recorded dwelling characteristics. This was an association and did not establish that mould caused the reported symptoms.

Among 558 occupants in the 246 dwellings with paired summer records, each additional 100 bedroom overheating hours at follow-up was associated with a prevalence ratio of 1.18 for sleep disturbed by respiratory symptoms, 95% CI 1.04–1.34. The model included baseline symptom status and measured confounders. Bedroom-level exposure was assigned to participating occupants using reported sleeping arrangements; it did not capture exposure away from home.

The renovation analysis used the 276 retained dwellings and adjusted for baseline conditions, provider and measured household differences. Relative to comparison dwellings, renovation was associated with an adjusted change in winter living-room temperature of +1.3 °C, 95% CI +0.8 to +1.8 °C. The corresponding adjusted change in occupied-bedroom median carbon dioxide was −145 ppm, 95% CI −220 to −70 ppm. These are differences in changes between groups, not simple changes within renovated dwellings.

For four-week wheeze among the 612 retained occupants, the adjusted renovation-associated difference in changes was −3.8 percentage points, 95% CI −8.1 to +0.5 percentage points. The interval includes no difference. The result does not establish a reduction in wheeze. Adjustment and weighting reduced some measured imbalances but could not remove unmeasured differences in renovation allocation or household behaviour.

## Occupant accounts and interpretation

The qualitative component comprised 36 interviews with adult occupants or caregivers and eight interviews with housing staff. Sampling included renovated and comparison dwellings and households with and without recorded mould. Two researchers coded transcripts using a shared framework, discussed discrepant cases and maintained an audit trail. Interview findings were not converted into population percentages.

Occupants described warmer winter rooms alongside uncertainty about ventilation controls, concern about electricity costs and difficulty distinguishing normal condensation from persistent dampness. Some reported avoiding opening windows because of noise or perceived security risks. Housing staff described delays between reporting dampness and identifying its underlying cause. These accounts helped explain variation in measured conditions but did not independently establish treatment effects.

## Limitations and completed outputs

The main limitations were non-random allocation, attrition, self-reported symptoms, incomplete personal exposure measurement and possible changes in occupancy between waves. The study had only one baseline and one follow-up observation period for renovation comparisons, so parallel pre-intervention trends could not be tested. Outdoor weather adjustment did not eliminate every seasonal difference. The particulate subsample was selected for logistical coverage and should not be treated as representative of all participating dwellings.

Completed outputs included the analysis dataset, an exposure-quality manual, a tenant-facing explanation of monitoring results and a maintenance briefing on ventilation handover and dampness reporting. The project measured health outcomes but did not test medicinal products. It did not measure network heat flows, storage performance, district-level energy balances or tariff impacts.
