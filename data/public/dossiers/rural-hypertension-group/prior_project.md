# Final scientific report: Veylorn Rural Medication and Pressure Study

**V-RMAPS; reporting period 1 January 2021–31 December 2024**  
Principal investigator: Adrelisse Pellavreth, PhD, MPH  
Host: Veylorn Ridge Academic Health Center  
Funder: Veylorn Community Care Research Trust, VCCRT-20-047  
Final report accepted: 28 February 2025  
Archive copy and closeout status reviewed: 30 September 2026

## Purpose and setting

V-RMAPS evaluated pharmacist-led blood-pressure management embedded in rural adult primary care. The study addressed medication reconciliation, treatment adjustment under collaborative practice agreements, and practical barriers to follow-up. Celdavreth Trace Community Clinics contributed eight clinics: four in West Virginia and four in Kentucky. These clinics served rural catchment areas and used the same electronic health record platform, although staffing patterns and appointment capacity differed.

The project was a pragmatic cluster-randomized clinical trial. Clinics were allocated in a 1:1 ratio, with four assigned to pharmacist-led management and four to usual care. Allocation was stratified by state, giving two clinics per study arm in each state. An independent statistician generated the allocation sequence after clinic participation agreements were signed. The protocol and primary analysis specification were deposited in the health center's dated research archive before allocation.

## Participants and procedures

Recruitment ran from 1 July 2021 to 30 June 2022. The study enrolled 480 patients, with 60 at each clinic and 240 in each arm. Participants were aged 30–79 years, had a recorded hypertension diagnosis, and had uncontrolled blood pressure confirmed at a research measurement visit. The age eligibility criteria excluded adolescents and adults younger than 30. Recruitment used clinic lists, clinician referrals, telephone invitations, and community health worker follow-up. Schools were not recruitment sites or referral partners.

The intervention offered pharmacist medication review and management during a six-month follow-up period. Pharmacists could recommend or implement medication changes within their signed collaborative practice agreements. Community health workers assisted with appointment reminders, transport arrangements, and communication about medication access. Usual-care clinics continued their existing clinical services. Both groups received the same research measurement schedule; neither participants nor clinical staff were blinded to allocation.

The primary endpoint was standardized seated systolic blood pressure at six months, adjusted for baseline systolic blood pressure and the state allocation stratum. Trained research assistants obtained three upper-arm readings after five minutes of seated rest and averaged the last two. Cuff size was selected from measured arm circumference. Staff documented recent smoking, caffeine consumption, and deviations from the measurement procedure. Whenever staffing allowed, the endpoint assessor was someone other than the participant's community health worker, but assessor masking was not assured.

The final six-month assessment window closed on 31 January 2023. Record reconciliation, qualitative analysis, and workflow assessment continued during the remaining award period. The quantitative database was locked on 30 September 2024.

## Participant accounting and blood-pressure results

Standardized six-month measurements were obtained for 408 of 480 (85.0%) participants. Follow-up was completed by 208 of 240 (86.7%) in the pharmacist arm and 200 of 240 (83.3%) in usual care. The Wilson 95% confidence interval for overall retention was 81.5%–87.9%. The 72 participants without a standardized endpoint comprised 68 participants alive at six months with missing measurements and four who died before six months. Among the 68 survivors with missing measurements, 36 could not be contacted, 20 declined the endpoint visit, and 12 moved outside the service area. Deaths were recorded as all-cause terminal events, distinct from missing measurements among survivors; the trial was not designed to estimate mortality effects.

Baseline systolic blood pressure averaged 153.2 mmHg, with a standard deviation of 12.4 mmHg, across all 480 participants. Arm-specific baseline means were 153.6 mmHg in pharmacist clinics and 152.8 mmHg in usual-care clinics. Among participants with paired research measurements, the unadjusted mean change was −11.4 mmHg in the pharmacist arm and −5.1 mmHg in usual care. These descriptive changes use the retained participants and are not the primary between-arm effect estimate.

The adjusted between-arm difference in six-month systolic blood pressure was −6.0 mmHg (95% CI −9.8 to −2.2), favoring pharmacist-led management. The prespecified linear mixed model included a clinic random intercept and used a small-sample degrees-of-freedom correction. Participants were analyzed according to their clinic's allocation. The primary estimate used the 408 observed standardized endpoints and represents an adjusted contrast among participants who survived and attended the endpoint assessment, rather than a treatment effect for all 480 enrollees.

The four deaths were handled as terminal events and were not assigned or imputed six-month blood-pressure values. The multiple-imputation sensitivity analysis was restricted to the 476 participants alive at six months, imputing the 68 missing measurements among those survivors. It estimated a survivor-conditional adjusted contrast of −5.5 mmHg (95% CI −9.6 to −1.4). Imputation included clinic, allocation, baseline blood pressure, age, and available routine follow-up measurements. This analysis assumed missingness was explainable by the observed variables among survivors. It did not establish robustness to unmeasured reasons for nonattendance. Both this contrast and the observed-endpoint analysis may be affected by post-randomization survival and attendance selection and do not estimate a treatment effect for all 480 enrollees. Neither estimate establishes a reduction in cardiovascular events.

## Records, interviews, and workflow findings

Electronic record linkage succeeded for 456 of 480 (95.0%) participants. Of the 24 unresolved linkages, 16 involved inactive legacy records and eight involved identifier discrepancies that could not be resolved within the approved linkage procedure. Successful linkage meant a verified match to a clinical record; it did not mean that every follow-up field was complete or that a standardized endpoint had been obtained. Routine blood-pressure readings were not substituted for missing primary endpoints.

The qualitative component included 32 individual interviews: 20 patients, six clinic staff members, and six community health workers. Sampling sought variation in attendance and clinic location rather than statistical representativeness. Participants described transport dependence, uncertainty about medication costs, difficulty reaching the clinic by telephone, and discomfort discussing missed doses. Staff described repeated reconciliation work when medication lists differed between clinical encounters. These were interpreted themes, not prevalence estimates. Interviews were recorded with consent, transcribed, and coded using a shared framework with documented disagreement resolution.

A separate workflow exercise involved 18 clinic staff members using a paper prototype for reconciling community blood-pressure records. In the first session, 12 of 18 (66.7%) completed the matching task without assistance. After revisions to labels and field order, 16 of 18 (88.9%) completed it without assistance in a second session. The same staff attended both sessions. These descriptive results are susceptible to practice effects and do not demonstrate improved patient blood pressure. No treatment allocation or medication changes were part of this workflow exercise.

## Limitations and closeout

The trial included only eight clinics within one network. Shared leadership and a common record system limit transferability to independently governed practices. Patient and staff masking was infeasible, and differences in contact intensity may have influenced follow-up. The retention difference and the small number of clusters constrain interpretation despite the prespecified analysis and sensitivity assessment.

The study measured office blood pressure and care processes. It did not collect objective sleep measurements, sleep diaries, sleep timing, sleep duration, or sleep regularity. It also did not collect research-grade physical activity monitoring, ambulatory blood pressure, or a biospecimen collection. The project provides no adolescent data and no evidence about recruiting through schools.

As of 30 September 2026, intervention delivery and recruitment remain ended. The award account is closed, while approved retention and secondary analysis of study records continue. No additional participants or follow-up measurements have been added since the database lock. The published main analysis, interview paper, linkage paper, and the separately identified workflow manuscript are documented in the publications list.
